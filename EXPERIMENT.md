# FF Utopia: the experiment is the artwork

> **FF Utopia is not only a club supporting artists, or a group making art *about* Utopia. FF Utopia is itself an evolving, collective artwork: an experiment in bringing Utopia into the present by practising it.**

The work is not a picture of a finished world. It is an attempt to create fragments of a better one, here and now, among people and other intelligences who do not necessarily agree about what that world should be.

This is both an **artistic proposition** and a **working question**. We do not know in advance whether our way of working will succeed.

## What is the artwork?

**The medium** is the collective's lived organisation: its relationships, artworks, invitations, acts of mutual help, distribution of resources, decisions, disagreements, and changes of mind. Grant applications, studio visits, shared exhibitions, member-led projects, and even the way we maintain this repository can be part of the work.

**The artists** are the participants who choose to shape it. Each also remains an autonomous maker of their own work. Joining a club does not transfer ownership of an artist's practice to FF Utopia.

**The form** is open-ended and decentralised. There is no single artist directing everyone's work, no final blueprint to illustrate, and no requirement that members share an ideology. Different visions can meet, challenge one another, or make useful things together without becoming identical.

**The audience** may witness, question, interpret, or—by invitation and choice—participate in the work. Public events and exhibitions are not merely showcases for the experiment; they can be *episodes within it*.

**The duration** is as long as participants continue making, maintaining, questioning, and changing the work. The artwork has a history, but no predetermined final state.

## What are we testing?

FF Utopia begins with a proposal: perhaps some aspects of a desired future become more possible when people **start practising them before that future exists**.

That raises questions we can explore through artistic action:

- Can autonomous people with different, even incompatible, ideas of Utopia cooperate without one vision taking control?
- Does sharing opportunities, criticism, audiences, knowledge, and resources improve each participant's ability to keep making art?
- Can the way we organise an exhibition, distribute a grant, or welcome a new member embody the values expressed in the art?
- When competing visions meet, can the encounter produce new understanding rather than forced agreement?
- What new exclusions, dependencies, burdens, or concentrations of power might our own attempted Utopia create?

These are questions, not claims that the club has already answered.

## How a play becomes part of the experiment

1. **Imagine.** A participant names something they believe belongs in a better world.
2. **Practise.** They make a concrete, preferably reversible play: a work, a shared resource, a new relationship, an exhibition, a way of deciding, or a different economic arrangement.
3. **Invite.** Others participate freely and on terms they understand. Disagreement is welcome; endorsement is not presumed.
4. **Observe.** Notice what actually happened. What was made? Whose possibilities expanded? Whose narrowed? What did the play cost?
5. **Learn and change.** Make the lesson, the uncertainty, and any correction visible where appropriate; let the next play differ.

A successful exhibition or grant does not by itself prove the proposition. Neither is a failed collaboration merely an embarrassment to conceal. **Unexpected outcomes, conflicts, and mistakes can become material for the artwork—provided we address their real consequences rather than aestheticising harm.**

This is **artistic experimentation**, not a claim to have performed a controlled scientific experiment or established that Utopia exists. We can still keep honest records, challenge our assumptions, and learn from counterexamples.

## What makes it visible?

A club can exhibit individual members' work, but it can also make aspects of its **own process** perceptible: the choices behind a project, the passing of an opportunity, a disagreement that changed a plan, a repair after something went wrong, or a relationship that enabled an artwork to exist.

A matchday can therefore show two works at once:

1. What individual artists made.
2. What the collective made possible—and what making it required.

We can document plays, projects, reflections, and changes in this repository, in exhibitions, in performances, or in other forms the participants invent. Documentation is selective and consent-based: not every conversation, conflict, financial detail, or participant needs to be made public to make the art real.

## Not an excuse to ignore reality

Calling the collective an artwork does **not** exempt it from responsibility. Nobody is automatically a performer, research subject, spokesperson, or contributor just because they interact with the club. Personal boundaries, authorship, fair compensation, privacy, safety, informed participation, and obligations to one another remain real.

If our actions contradict the future we say we want, that contradiction matters. It should be possible to name it, try to repair it, and revise the work.

FF Utopia is an artwork that can be *wrong about Utopia*. Its ability to notice and respond to that possibility is part of the experiment.
